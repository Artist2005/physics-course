Acid-base titration curves, buffer capacity, and indicator selection

Calculating aqueous pH at four benchmark regions along a titration curve turns complex equilibrium problems into a predictable sequence. This guide compares strong and weak acid titrations, explains buffer resistance, and works through four quantitative problems.

Four benchmark regions of every titration curve

A titration curve tracks solution pH against added titrant volume. Four distinct equilibrium regimes govern its geometry: the initial analyte before titrant addition, the pre-equivalence buffer zone, the sharp equivalence-point inflection, and the post-equivalence excess titrant region.

When titrating strong acid HCl with strong base NaOH, the resulting salt NaCl does not hydrolyze, placing the equivalence point strictly at pH = 7.00 at 25 °C inside a steep vertical jump from roughly pH 4 to pH 10. By contrast, titrating weak acetic acid CH₃COOH with strong base produces sodium acetate at equivalence. Because acetate anions accept protons from water to generate OH⁻, the equivalence point shifts alkaline above pH 8.5 and the vertical inflection narrows.

Buffer zone behavior and the half-equivalence point

Between initial base addition and full neutralization of weak acid HA, both unreacted acid and conjugate base A⁻ coexist in solution. The mixture resists pH change according to the Henderson-Hasselbalch relation pH = pKa + log([A⁻] / [HA]). Buffer capacity peaks at the half-equivalence point where exactly half of the weak acid has been converted to conjugate base, making [A⁻] = [HA] and pH = pKa. Chemists routinely read unknown acid dissociation constants directly from this midpoint. Compare buffered and unbuffered pH response visually in the 3D Buffer Solutions Lab.

Choosing the right visual indicator

An acid-base indicator is itself a weak organic acid HInd whose protonated and deprotonated forms absorb different wavelengths. Its color transition spans roughly pKInd ± 1. Accurate endpoint detection requires that this transition range fall squarely inside the vertical equivalence jump of the titration curve.

For a weak acid titrated with strong base, equivalence occurs near pH 8.7, making phenolphthalein with transition 8.2-10.0 an ideal choice while methyl orange at 3.1-4.4 turns color far too early. Conversely, titrating weak base ammonia NH₃ with strong acid places equivalence near pH 5.3, where methyl red or methyl orange tracks the endpoint accurately. Four worked analytical chemistry problems

Problem 1. Determining unknown acid molarity from endpoint volume

Exactly 25.0 mL of aqueous HCl requires 20.0 mL of 0.150 mol/L NaOH to reach equivalence. Calculate acid molarity.

Because stoichiometry in HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O is 1 : 1, molar equality holds as CaVa = CbVb. Solving gives Ca = (0.150 · 20.0) / 25.0 = 0.120 mol/L. Volume units cancel cleanly as long as both volumes share milliliters.

Problem 2. Buffer pH shift after strong acid addition

One liter of acetate buffer holds 0.20 mol acetic acid at pKa = 4.76 and 0.20 mol sodium acetate. Find the new pH after adding 0.02 mol gaseous HCl at constant volume.

Strong acid protonates acetate quantitatively, decreasing CH₃COO⁻ to 0.18 mol and increasing CH₃COOH to 0.22 mol. Substituting into Henderson-Hasselbalch yields pH = 4.76 + log(0.18 / 0.22) = 4.76 − 0.087 = 4.67. Adding the same 0.02 mol of HCl to unbuffered pure water would plunge pH from 7.00 down to 1.70.

Problem 3. Equivalence-point pH of weak-acid titration

Compute the pH obtained by mixing equal volumes of 0.20 mol/L CH₃COOH and 0.20 mol/L NaOH at 25 °C.

Doubling total volume halves the resulting sodium acetate concentration to C = 0.10 mol/L. The base hydrolysis constant of acetate equals Kb = Kw / Ka = 10⁻¹⁴ / (1.74 × 10⁻⁵) = 5.75 × 10⁻¹⁰. Equilibrium hydroxide concentration is [OH⁻] = √(Kb · C) = 7.58 × 10⁻⁶ mol/L, giving pOH = 5.12 and pH = 14.00 − 5.12 = 8.88. Problem 4. Diprotic acid neutralization stoichiometry

What volume of 0.25 mol/L NaOH is needed to completely neutralize 40.0 mL of 0.15 mol/L sulfuric acid H₂SO₄?

The balanced equation H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O requires two moles of hydroxide per mole of acid: n(NaOH) = 2 · 0.15 · 0.040 = 0.012 mol. Dividing by titrant molarity gives V = 0.012 / 0.25 = 0.048 L = 48.0 mL.
