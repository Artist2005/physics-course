LABORATORY METHODS · UNCERTAINTY PROPAGATION · AP, IB & UNIVERSITY

Experimental error analysis in school and university science laboratories

A numerical measurement without an uncertainty bound cannot confirm or refute a physical law. This guide explains how to combine instrumental tolerance with random scatter, propagate uncertainty through derived equations, and interpret linear regression plots.

Direct measurements: combining instrumental and random uncertainties

Every reading taken with a ruler, caliper, voltmeter, or stopwatch carries two distinct error components. Instrumental uncertainty Δxinst reflects scale resolution: half the smallest graduation on an analog meter stick or dial, or the rated least-digit accuracy of a digital display.

Random uncertainty arises from reaction-time jitter, air drafts, and slight release variations. To quantify it, repeat the measurement n times under identical conditions, typically five to ten trials. Compute the sample mean x̄ = (∑ xi) / n and Bessel's unbiased standard error of the mean sx̄ = √(∑(xi − x̄)² / [n(n − 1)]). Multiply sx̄ by the two-sided Student factor tP, n−1 to obtain the random confidence half-width Δxrand. Combine both independent sources in quadrature via Δx = √(Δxrand² + Δxinst²), or compute them directly in our Bessel and Student-t calculator.

Propagating uncertainty through indirect equations

Most laboratory objectives determine a derived constant from multiple measured variables. For any power-law expression y = C · ap · bq / cr with independent inputs, fractional uncertainties combine in quadrature weighted by their exponents: εy = √((p · εa)² + (q · εb)² + (r · εc)²), where εa = Δa / |a|.

Inspecting exponents immediately reveals where to invest experimental care. Any variable entering squared contributes double weight to total fractional error. That is why timing twenty or thirty consecutive oscillations of a pendulum instead of a single swing divides human stopwatch reaction error by thirty and makes length measurement competitive with timing precision.

Worked laboratory dataset: measuring gravitational acceleration g

Pendulum timing series and final confidence bound

A student measures pendulum length as L = 1.000 ± 0.002 m and records five timings of twenty full swings: 40.1 s, 39.9 s, 40.2 s, 40.0 s, and 40.3 s. The sample mean is t̄ = 40.10 s, yielding a single period T = 2.005 s. The sum of squared deviations equals 0.10 s², so standard error is st̄ = √(0.10 / 20) = 0.071 s. Multiplying by the 95% Student factor t = 2.78 for n = 5 gives Δtrand = 0.20 s, or ΔT = 0.010 s per oscillation.

Computing g = 4π²L / T² = 39.478 · 1.000 / 2.005² = 9.820 m/s². Fractional length uncertainty is 0.20 %, whereas twice the fractional period uncertainty is 1.00 %. Adding in quadrature yields 1.02 %, corresponding to Δg = 0.10 m/s². The final reported measurement is g = 9.82 ± 0.10 m/s², which cleanly brackets the standard value 9.81 m/s². Linearizing curves and interpreting R² in regression plots

Nonlinear laws such as T = 2π√(L/g) become far easier to verify when plotted in linearized coordinates, here T² versus L. The slope of the fitted line equals 4π² / g, and a nonzero vertical intercept immediately exposes a systematic zero-point offset in how string length was measured to the bob's center of mass. Inside the 3D lab measurement logger, recording five points across a slider plots the least-squares fit alongside the coefficient of determination R², helping distinguish linear scaling from quadratic or exponential trends.

Significant-figure rounding rules for laboratory reports

Round final absolute uncertainty Δx upward to one significant digit, keeping two significant digits only when the leading digit is 1 or 2. Then round the mean value x̄ to the exact same decimal place. Reporting mismatched precision such as 9.8234 ± 0.1 m/s² implies meaningless digits beyond the error bound; write 9.8 ± 0.1 m/s² or 9.82 ± 0.10 m/s² instead.
