Archimedes' Principle & Flotation Conditions

A body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the submerged portion of the body.

Question
A solid cube of density ρbody = 600 kg/m3 is placed into fresh water (ρwater = 1000 kg/m3). What fraction of the cube's volume will be submerged in equilibrium?
Selected answer Option B
In static equilibrium of a floating body, gravity is balanced by the Archimedes buoyant force: ρbody g V0 = ρfluid g Vsub. Hence the submerged fraction is Vsub/V0 = ρbody/ρfluid = 600/1000 = 0.6 (60%). If ρbody > ρfluid, the body sinks.

Core Curriculum · Grade 7
Mechanism

Hydrostatic pressure in a stationary fluid of density ρfluid increases linearly with depth: p(h) = p0 + ρfluid g h. Because the upward pressure on the bottom surface of a submerged body exceeds the downward pressure on its top, integrating pressure forces over the body's surface yields an upward Archimedean buoyant force FA.

Equations and conservation laws

By Archimedes' principle, the buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid: FA = ρfluid g Vsub. Vertical motion of a body of total volume V and density ρbody is governed by the balance between weight mg = ρbody g V, buoyancy FA, and fluid drag.

Measurements and applications

Three regimes emerge from the density ratio: 1) if **ρbody ρfluid**, it sinks to the bottom with apparent weight Wapp = (ρbody - ρfluid)gV.

Experimental parameters and SI units
  • Body density ρbody: 200 … 1800 kg/m³, standard value 600 kg/m³
  • Fluid density ρfluid: 700 … 2000 kg/m³, standard value 1000 kg/m³
  • Cube side length a: 0.8 … 1.6 m, standard value 1 m
  • Fluid viscosity η: 0.1 … 1.2 Pa·s, standard value 0.35 Pa·s
Physical model and application
The flotation of steel vessels is governed by the ratio of average hull density to fluid density: the hydrostatic pressure difference between the bottom and top surfaces of a submerged body generates an upward Archimedes buoyant force equal to the weight of displaced fluid.
