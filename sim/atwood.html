Atwood's Machine: Dynamics of Connected Masses & Pulley Inertia

Two weights connected by an inextensible thread over a pulley move with a single common acceleration determined by the difference in weight and total system inertia.

Question
On an ideal massless pulley (mpulley = 0), two loads m1 = 3 kg and m2 = 1 kg are suspended. What is the acceleration a of the system and how does thread tension T compare to the weights of the loads?
Selected answer Option B
Writing Newton's second law for both loads: m1 g - T1 = m1 a and T2 - m2 g = m2 a, plus the pulley torque equation (T1 - T2)R = Iε = (1)/(2)mpulleyR2(a/R), yields a = ((m1 - m2)g)/(m1 + m2 + (1)/(2)mpulley). For the descending heavier load m1, tension is less than its weight (T1 = m1(g - a) m2 g).

Core Curriculum · Grade 9-10
Mechanism

Invented by George Atwood in 1784, Atwood's machine is a classic apparatus for verifying Newton's laws of motion under "diluted gravity." Two masses m1 and m2 connected by an inextensible cord over a pulley of radius R and mass Mp share a common linear acceleration a = α R.

Equations and conservation laws

Writing Newton's second law for both masses and the rotational torque equation for the solid disk pulley (I = (1)/(2)Mp R2) gives m1 g - T1 = m1 a, T2 - m2 g = m2 a, and (T1 - T2)R = Iα = (1)/(2)Mp R a. Summing the three equations yields the system acceleration **a = ((m1 - m2)g)/(m1 + m2 + (1)/(2)Mp)**.

Measurements and applications

Whenever the pulley has inertia (Mp > 0), the cord tensions on the two sides are not equal (T1 ≠ T2), because a net torque (T1 - T2)R is required to angularly accelerate the pulley! Choosing m1 ≈ m2 slows the motion (a ≪ g) for accurate timing.

Experimental parameters and SI units
  • Left load mass m₁: 1 … 5 kg, standard value 3.2 kg
  • Right load mass m₂: 1 … 5 kg, standard value 1.8 kg
  • Pulley mass Mp: 0 … 4 kg, standard value 1 kg
  • Pulley radius R: 0.5 … 1.2 m, standard value 0.8 m
Physical model and application
An elevator with a counterweight is a direct engineering application of Atwood's machine. Because masses m1 and m2 hang on opposite ends of a common cable over a pulley, only their weight difference (m1 - m2)g drives the motion, yielding a controlled, reduced acceleration.
