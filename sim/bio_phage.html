Bacteriophage T4 Structure & Lytic Cycle: Biomechanical Syringe & Viral DNA Injection

"Bacteriophage T4 operates as a spring-loaded bionanomechanical syringe: upon docking its tail fibers onto Escherichia coli surface receptors, its helical sheath contracts and injects viral DNA under ~50 atm pressure while leaving the protein coat outside."

Question
In the landmark 1952 Hershey-Chase blender experiment, bacteriophages were differentially labeled with radioactive phosphorus 32P (incorporated exclusively into DNA) and radioactive sulfur 35S (incorporated exclusively into capsid proteins). Which isotope entered the infected bacterial cells, and what fundamental principle did this prove?
Selected answer Option B
Bacteriophage T4 is a complex virus infecting *Escherichia coli*, comprising an elongated icosahedral protein head (capsid) packed with double-stranded DNA (approx 169 kbp) pressurized to **P ≈ 40..60 atm, coupled to a contractile tail apparatus. When the six long tail fibers bind lipopolysaccharide (LPS) receptors on the host surface, the hexagonal baseplate switches conformation, the outer sheath contracts to less than half its length, and the rigid inner tube (tipped with lysozyme domains) punctures the cell envelope, injecting viral DNA into the cytoplasm. In the Hershey-Chase experiment (1952), radioactive 32P (DNA) pelleted inside the bacteria and appeared in progeny phages, whereas 35S (protein capsid)** remained in the supernatant after agitation, establishing DNA as the genetic material.

Mechanism

Bacteriophage T4 is a tailed double-stranded DNA virus (*Myoviridae*) that infects *Escherichia coli* and is a classic example of bionanomechanical engineering. Its virion comprises a prolate icosahedral protein capsid (115× 85 nm), a collar, a rigid inner tail tube surrounded by a contractile helical sheath, and a hexagonal baseplate bearing six short pins and six long receptor-binding tail fibers.

Equations and conservation laws

Packed inside the capsid by a powerful ATP-driven portal motor is a 169 kbp (56 μm) dsDNA genome whose bending stiffness and electrostatic repulsion generate an internal pressure of **P0 ≈ 50 atm**. When tail fibers lock onto host lipopolysaccharide (LPS) receptors, the baseplate undergoes a hexagon-to-star conformational switch, contracting the sheath by 55% to drive the lysozyme-tipped core through the bacterial envelope and inject the DNA.

Measurements and applications

In the classic 1952 Hershey-Chase experiment, phage DNA was labeled with radioactive phosphorus (**32P) and capsid proteins with radioactive sulfur (35S). Only 32P-labeled DNA** entered the host bacteria to direct progeny virion synthesis, proving that DNA is the hereditary material.

Physical model and application
Bacteriophage T4 looks and acts like a lunar lander equipped with a high-pressure syringe. Landing on six tail fibers, it contracts its sheath and injects its DNA genome pressurized to 50 atm into the bacterium while leaving its empty protein shell outside.
