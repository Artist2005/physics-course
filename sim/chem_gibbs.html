Gibbs Free Energy ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, Entropy & Reaction Spontaneity

Nature strives simultaneously toward two opposing goals: minimum enthalpy (ΔH 0) at high temperatures.

Question
Under what thermodynamic condition is an endothermic chemical reaction (Δ H > 0) spontaneous (Δ G < 0) at constant temperature and pressure?

Advanced Level · AP / University · Chemical Thermodynamics
Mechanism

At constant temperature T and pressure P, chemical spontaneity is governed by the Gibbs free energy Δ G = Δ H - TΔ S, which balances the enthalpic driving force (Δ H, heat of bond rearrangement) against the entropic driving force (TΔ S, disorder and microstate multiplicity).

Equations and conservation laws

A reaction is spontaneous when Δ G 0, and at chemical equilibrium when Δ G = 0. The standard free energy change determines the equilibrium constant through the van 't Hoff isotherm: Δ G° = -RT ln Keq.

Measurements and applications

When Δ H and Δ S share the same sign, spontaneity reverses across the inversion temperature T^* = (Δ H°)/(Δ S°): an endothermic reaction with positive entropy change (Δ H > 0, Δ S > 0) becomes spontaneous at high temperatures T > T^*.

Experimental parameters and SI units
  • Reaction enthalpy ΔH° (− exo, + endo): -120 … 120 kJ/mol, standard value 60 kJ/mol
  • Reaction entropy ΔS°: -200 … 250 J/(mol·K), standard value 150 J/(mol·K)
  • Absolute temperature T: 200 … 1000 K, standard value 500 K
  • System pressure P: 0.5 … 5 atm, standard value 1 atm
Physical model and application
The spontaneity of a chemical process at constant temperature and pressure is governed by the Gibbs free energy change Δ G = Δ H - TΔ S, which balances enthalpic (Delta H) and entropic (TΔ S) driving forces: at low temperatures the enthalpy term dominates, whereas at high temperatures a positive entropy change (Δ S > 0) drives endothermic reactions forward (Δ G < 0).
