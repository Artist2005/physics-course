Dispersion of Light & Spectrum Formation in a Prism

A narrow beam of white light in a glass prism splits into a rainbow spectrum because short violet waves have a lower phase velocity in glass than long red waves.

Question
A white light beam passes through a triangular glass prism. Rays of which color are refracted through the largest deviation angle δ and why?

Core Curriculum · Grade 9-11
Mechanism

Optical dispersion is the dependence of a medium's refractive index n(λ) on wavelength λ. In transparent dielectrics such as crown or flint glass (normal dispersion), n(λ) increases as wavelength decreases according to Cauchy's equation: n(λ) = A + (B)/(λ2) + (C)/(λ4), driven by ultraviolet electronic resonances of the material.

Equations and conservation laws

Tracing a ray through a triangular prism of apex angle A requires two Snell's-law refractions: sin α1 = n sin γ1, γ1 + γ2 = A, and sin α2 = n sin γ2. The total angular deviation from the original beam direction is δ(λ) = α1 + α2 - A (or δ ≈ (n(λ) - 1)A for small apex angles).

Measurements and applications

Because short-wavelength violet light (λ ≈ 400 nm) travels slower in glass and experiences a higher refractive index than red light (nviolet > nred), violet rays deviate most strongly (δviolet > δred), fanning white light out into Newton's continuous rainbow spectrum.

Experimental parameters and SI units
  • Prism apex angle φ: 35 … 62 °, standard value 52 °
  • Incident beam angle α₁: 25 … 65 °, standard value 44 °
  • Base glass index n₀: 1.42 … 1.72, standard value 1.5
  • Cauchy dispersion coeff. B: 0.02 … 0.12 μm², standard value 0.06 μm²
Physical model and application
Atmospheric rainbows and spectral splitting in an optical prism arise from normal dispersion: polychromatic white light is a superposition of different wavelengths. In glass, shorter-wavelength violet waves travel slower than red waves and refract more strongly, fanning the beam into a spectrum.
