Converging Lens & Light Wavefront Focusing

An optical lens delays the central portion of a light wavefront more than its edges, transforming an incident plane wavefront into a spherical wavefront converging at the focus.

Question
A parallel light beam with a plane wavefront strikes a biconvex glass lens. Why does the plane wavefront become spherical and converge to a single point (principal focus F) after passing through the lens?
Selected answer Option B
By Fermat's principle and Huygens' principle, the optical path length L = n· l for all rays from the plane wavefront to focus F is equal. Because the lens is thickest along the optical axis, the central part of the wavefront travels through glass at speed v = c/n for a longer distance than peripheral rays. Consequently, the plane wavefront bends into a concave spherical surface centered at the principal focus F = (R)/(2(n-1)).
Formula
F = (R)/(2(n - 1)), D = (1)/(F) [dpt], f = (d· F)/(d - F)

Core Curriculum · Grade 9-11
Mechanism

A biconvex converging lens acts as a spatial phase transformer for light wavefronts. Because the glass (n > 1) is thickest along the optical axis, the central portion of an incident wavefront travels slower (v = c/n) over a longer path than the outer edges, bending a diverging or plane wavefront into a converging spherical wavefront.

Equations and conservation laws

Optical power D = 1/F (in diopters, m-1) is given by the Lensmaker's equation D = (n - 1)((1)/(R1) + (1)/(R2)). Object distance d, image distance f, and focal length F satisfy the Gaussian thin-lens equation (1)/(d) + (1)/(f) = (1)/(F) ⇒ f = (dF)/(d - F), with lateral magnification Γ = (|f|)/(d).

Measurements and applications

Varying object distance d relative to F and 2F transitions across three optical regimes: for d > 2F the image is real, inverted, and diminished (camera sensor / human eye); for F < d < 2F it is real, inverted, and magnified (projector); and for d < F rays diverge to form a virtual, upright, magnified image (magnifying glass).

Experimental parameters and SI units
  • Lens refractive index n: 1.35 … 1.9, standard value 1.55
  • Surface curvature radius R: 2.2 … 5.2 m, standard value 3.4 m
  • Light beam aperture H: 0.8 … 2.1 m, standard value 1.6 m
  • Light wavelength λ: 410 … 680 nm, standard value 510 nm
Physical model and application
In microscope objectives, camera lenses, and the crystalline lens of the eye, a converging lens is thicker at its center: the central portion of an incident wavefront traverses more glass and undergoes a greater phase delay, curving a planar wavefront into a spherical wave that converges at the focus.
